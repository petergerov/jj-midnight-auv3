## The product name

**Status: decided — `jj-midnight`.** It is the only candidate found that does
what `jj-breeze` does and `jj-tulsa` cannot: pay off twice, as "After
Midnight" for anyone who knows Cale and as *late, dark, quiet* for everyone
else. Its weakness is known and accepted — it says nothing about guitar and
names no block of the chain, so the subtitle and keyword field carry the
category, as set out in *Where the category words go*. The reasoning, and the
two collisions that were checked and cleared, are under `jj-midnight` in
*Also in view* below; the rest of the list is kept as the record of how the
decision was reached.

Two things still open, in order:

1. **A real trademark clearance.** What was done is a web search, not a
   clearance. Focusrite's Midnight Plugin Suite has been out of commerce
   since 2020 and unobtainable since, but that is a finding, not an opinion
   from a lawyer.
2. **Renaming the GitHub repository and the Pages site**, which are still
   `jj-tulsa` and are the one thing that cannot be changed from inside the
   working tree. The README and the site already link to the `jj-midnight`
   URLs, so those links 404 until the repository is renamed. GitHub redirects
   the old URLs to the new one afterwards, so nothing that points at the old
   name breaks.

The rename itself is **done** — see *What the rename covered* below.

### What a name has to do

Three requirements, in order of how often they get forgotten.

**1. Work without the reference.** Look at why `jj-breeze` works: "breeze"
reads as *light, airy, unhurried* to anyone who has never heard of J.J. Cale —
and **additionally** as "Call Me The Breeze" to anyone who has. The name pays
off twice.

**2. Not fight the guitar context.** Most of the "feel" names below sit oddly
here: *simmer*, *haze*, *sway*, *shiver* could be a reverb, a synth, or a
photo filter. A name that hints at guitar is worth something.

But this requirement was first written as "tell this is for guitar players
*before* they read the subtitle", and that is too strict — the subtitle is on
the same store card as the name. The name does not have to carry the
category; the subtitle and keyword field do, and they are better at it. See
*Where the category words go* below.

**3. Say something about the sound.** Nice to have, not essential — but a name
that names a real block of the chain is doing free work in the listing.

### Why jj-tulsa is weak

Legally it is fine. Tulsa is a city name and is not registrable as an artist
mark; the exposure was always the store description, never the title.

It fails the first requirement, though. Someone who knows Cale gets
everything. Someone who does not — and by our own read of the market, that is
most of the people who actually buy AUv3 plug-ins — reads "a city in Oklahoma"
and nothing else. The name spends the store listing's first impression on the
smaller half of the audience. It fails the second requirement too: nothing
about it says guitar.

### Shortlist

Ranked against the three requirements above. Drawbacks recorded so they are
not rediscovered in three months. Everything else is in the long list below —
nothing has been dropped.

1. **`Underwound`** — carries all three requirements at once. A real
   pickup-winding term (fewer turns: clear, dynamic, warm) for anyone who
   knows, and plainly "not wound up" for everyone else: the same trick
   `jj-breeze` pulls. Strong enough to stand without the prefix, which is the
   one case where giving up the family looks worth it. *Drawback:* nine
   letters, and it needs the store subtitle to land the tone.
2. **`Rosewood`** — hits guitar and sound at the same time: the dark fretboard
   wood, and dark is literally what this chain does. Needs no explaining,
   sounds expensive. *Drawback:* a common word in gear copy, so it may not
   stand out in search — and worth a trademark check.
3. **`Sideman`** — the best fit to the actual thesis. A sideman plays behind
   the singer and never steps forward, which is what this plug-in does to a
   guitar. Human rather than technical, which almost nothing else here is.
   *Drawback:* guitar-adjacent rather than unmistakably guitar, and Wurlitzer
   sold a "Side Man" rhythm machine in 1959 — almost certainly lapsed, but
   check.
4. **`Low Watt`** — names the *cause* rather than the feeling, which nothing
   else on the list does. Tweed amps run five to fifteen watts, and that is
   precisely why they compress and break up early. Amp vocabulary, says
   "quiet", two short words. *Drawback:* reads as a spec rather than a name,
   and "low" in a product title is a hard sell.
5. **`jj-alnico`** — the most brandable. Sounds expensive, unmistakably
   guitar, and Alnico II already connotes soft and compressed in the trade.
   *Drawback:* technical; non-players get nothing from it.
6. **`jj-thumb`** — the most accurate to what this is for, because it names
   the playing style rather than the gear: the thumb is the instrument of
   this idiom, from Travis through Cale to Wes Montgomery. Five letters, so
   unlike `jj-thumbpick` it does not crowd the panel wordmark. *Drawback:*
   on its own "thumb" is anatomical before it is musical.
7. **`Class A`** — the shortest brandable option. The operating class of the
   small tweed amps: low power, early breakup, warm. Reads as amp vocabulary
   instantly. *Drawback:* also generic marketing jargon ("class A" as in
   first-rate), which dilutes it.
8. **`jj-sag`** — amp vocabulary for exactly what the compressor does, and
   plain English for "droops, relaxes". *Drawback:* "sag" has an unflattering
   everyday sense, and it is amp vocabulary rather than guitar vocabulary.
9. **`Slow Burn`** — the strongest image in the list, and both readings land
   (low gain, and unhurried). *Drawback:* "slow" in a product name can be
   misread as *sluggish software*.

**Also in view:**

- **`Heat Lightning`** — lightning without thunder: intensity without noise.
  The best metaphor found for low-gain breakup. Held back only on length —
  thirteen characters is tight on the panel.
- **`Cimarron`** — if the family breaks for a place rather than a term, break
  it for this. A river through Oklahoma: the right landscape without being the
  address. Opaque on a store shelf is the trade.
- **`jj-simmer`** — describes the drive block precisely, no second meaning
  working against it. Says nothing about guitar.
- **`jj-haze`** — the easiest word here to say, spell and remember, but for a
  *guitar* app it points at "Purple Haze" and therefore at fuzz, the opposite
  of this plug-in. That misdirection is why it is not higher.
- **`Last Set`** — musician vocabulary for exactly this feel: the final set of
  the night, everyone tired, nobody pushing. The second human name here after
  `Sideman`. Describes the playing rather than the gear, which is also its
  weakness — nothing about it says guitar.
- **`Gloaming`** — archaic for twilight. The most distinctive option, with no
  store competition at all. Needs explaining once; says nothing about guitar.
- **`Low Action`** — string height close to the fretboard: exact guitar
  vocabulary for an instrument that plays without effort, and plain English for
  "not much going on". Carries all three requirements. *Drawback:* a second
  "low" name, and it inherits `Low Watt`'s problem — "low" in a title reads as
  a deficiency rather than a quality.
- **`Tortoise`** — tortoiseshell was the pick material until the 1973 ban, and
  players still describe it as the warm, round-sounding one: right era and
  right tone claim at once, while everyone else reads "slow". *Drawback:*
  reads as an animal before it reads as a pick.
- **`jj-midnight`** — the one name found so far that does the `jj-breeze`
  trick outright. "After Midnight" is Cale's best-known song — the Clapton
  cover of it is what made his name — so the reference is there for anyone who
  has it, while everyone else reads *late, dark, quiet*, which is what this
  chain sounds like. Eight letters, two parts, easy to say and spell.
  *Drawback:* empty on the second and third requirements — nothing about
  guitar, nothing about a block of the chain. That is the real reservation,
  and it is the same gap `Gloaming` and `Cimarron` carry with less to show
  for it.

  *Collisions, checked:* two exist and neither disqualifies it. Focusrite's
  **Midnight Plugin Suite** was Midnight EQ and Midnight Compressor, modelling
  the ISA110 and ISA130 — but it is withdrawn entirely: last release February
  2020, development ceased, and Focusrite's own support page says it can no
  longer be downloaded or activated, the free-legacy offer having ended too.
  Roughly six years out of commerce, past the three-year presumption of
  abandonment for those goods, and no Focusrite registration for *Midnight*
  surfaced on a search that did return their Launchpad and Vocaster marks.
  **Midnight Plaza** is a current lo-fi AUv3 in the same store and category,
  but one niche app does not own a dictionary word, and nobody finds an AUv3
  by searching "midnight" — the name is what people search by choice, and the
  subtitle and keyword field catch the rest.

  Recorded at that weight because the first pass filed this under the wrong
  rule and dropped the name for it. It matches none of the three shapes in
  *Names to rule out*: **Bigsby / Dyna Comp / Ross** are coined distinctive
  marks on live products, where *midnight* is a dictionary word and gets thin
  protection; **Shimmer**'s harm is an expectation gap, the buyer promised a
  sound the product does not make, and there is no gap here since the word
  describes this chain correctly if anything; **Slack**'s harm is a competitor
  owning the search term outright, which Midnight Plaza does not. What is left
  is an engineer thinking "like the Focusrite one?" — cosmetic, and a
  flattering association rather than a misleading one. A proper trademark
  clearance still belongs on the pre-submission list: the above is a web
  search, not a clearance.
- **`Night Drive`** — was in *Names to rule out* while it was also a preset
  name; with the preset withdrawn the self-collision goes, and the search
  objection largely with it, since `Night Drive` is far more distinctive than
  `jj-drive`. Unprefixed it matches `Slow Burn`, `Last Set` and `Low Watt`,
  so the length objection goes too. What remains is the reason it stays here
  rather than moving up: *Night Drive* is an occupied aesthetic — Kavinsky,
  the *Drive* soundtrack, a genre's worth of retrowave releases — so for a
  guitar app it does not merely fail to say guitar, it actively says analog
  synth. That is the **Shimmer** structure, and the second requirement
  failing in its active form. In an audio-plug-in listing *drive* is a loaded
  word besides: the eye pulls it out of the phrase and reads overdrive, on a
  chain that stops at the edge of breakup by design. And unlike `jj-midnight`
  it carries no Cale reading, so the image pays off only once. *Cost to
  weigh:* Night Drive is one of the three presets the positioning aims at the
  20–35 buyers, and both `docs/space.md` and `docs/wobble.md` use it as their
  example setting.

### Long list

Grouped by where the word comes from, because the useful decision is which
*direction* to go rather than which word. Shortlisted names are omitted here.

**The feel** — mostly fails the guitar requirement: these could be a reverb, a
synth, or a photo filter. All of them name the *modulation*, which is the
block that says least about this plug-in; the compressor is the heart of it.
`jj-shades` (sunglasses, gradations of tone; also a Cale album, 1981) ·
`jj-sway` (the tremolo) · `jj-porch` (warm, rural; the mood not the sound) ·
`jj-shiver` (a real claim on the tremolo, but the word means trembling with
*cold* — wrong temperature for a warm, settled plug-in, and it suggests fast
nervous flutter where this sways slowly. `jj-sway` says the same thing without
the chill.)

**Pickups and magnets** — the richest seam, because these words already mean
*warm and old* rather than just *guitar*.
`Low Wind` (fewer turns on the coil, and reads as weather) ·
`Neck Pickup` / `jj-neck` (the position that *is* dark and round — every
player knows it, which is the point; but the second meaning is weak, and
`neck` alone is anatomical, the same problem as `jj-thumb`)

**Picking** — the technique behind this entire style. The idiom has more
names than `jj-thumb` suggests: *thumbpicking* is the general style,
*Travis picking* the alternating-bass pattern behind it, *dead thumb* the
steady muted bass of Texas and Delta blues, *thumb brush* the downward sweep
of the thumb pad across the strings, and a *roll* is the repeating arpeggio
figure (forward, backward, thumb-lead).
`Palm Mute` (the gesture of restraint, in pure guitar language) ·
`jj-hybrid` (pick and fingers together) ·
`Dead Thumb` (the closest term in the language to this tone — it names a
dark, muted, unchanging pulse — but it repeats `Dead Strings`' problem: the
word sells nothing on a store card) ·
`Thumb Brush` (the soft attack itself, unmistakably a guitar gesture, and
"brush" plainly means a light touch; reads as a grooming or paint tool at
first glance, and it names a technique rather than anything the plug-in does)
· `jj-roll` (short and brandable, guitar and banjo vocabulary, and plainly
unhurried motion — but "roll" is overloaded past usefulness: rock and roll,
drum roll, barrel roll)

**The setup** — how the instrument is set up and strung, which is half of why
this tone sounds the way it does.
`Dead Strings` (literally how the dark, thuddy version of this tone is got —

**Amp power** — the only angle that explains *why* the sound is like this.
`Five Watt` · `Half Power` · `Headroom` (names the actual cause — a small amp
has none, which is why it compresses and breaks up early — but it reads
backwards to most people, who take headroom to mean clean and loud)

**The tone control.**
`Rolled Off` (rolling the tone knob back is the most literal description of
this chain's darkness, but it is engineer vocabulary and sounds like a loss)

**The hand.**
`Light Touch` (how you play this style) · `Callus` ·
`jj-touch` (the strongest of the plain-English candidates on substance:

is an iPad and iPhone app, and on that platform "touch" is what you do to

**The wood.**

### Where the category words go

The name and the subtitle have different jobs, and trying to make the name do
both is what produces names like `jj-guitaramp`.

```
jj-sag
Vintage guitar chain · comp, amp, trem
```

The name is what someone remembers and searches for by choice; the subtitle
and the keyword field are what an unaimed search finds. Plain words like
*guitar*, *amp*, *compressor*, *tremolo* and *slapback* belong there, where
they cost nothing and are read by everyone who sees the name anyway.

Two things that must **not** go in the store listing, whatever else does: the
preset song titles, and any artist's name. See *Naming rules* below.

### Names to rule out

- **Slow Hand** — the obvious candidate in the two-word direction and the one
  that must never be used. It is Clapton's nickname: a *person*, not a song
  title, which is the category ruled out from the start.
- **Slack** — tempting, since *slack key* is a real tuning and "slack" means
  loose. Unwinnable in App Store search against Slack.
- **Sooner** — a University of Oklahoma trademark.
- **Red Dirt** — an actual Oklahoma music genre, so it reads as a scene label
  and implies affiliation.
- **Bigsby, Tremolux, Vibrolux, Bassman, Dyna Comp, Ross** — guitar vocabulary
  is full of model and company names that read as generic but are not.
  Historical gear names are the most common trap in this direction: check
  before falling for one.
- **Twang** — free to use, but it points the wrong way tonally: twang means
  bright and country, and this plug-in is dark.
- **jj-guitaramp**, and descriptive names generally — three separate problems.
  It claims a category the product cannot back: this is not an amp sim, and
  next to AmpliTube or BIAS FX a buyer expecting amp models, a gain range and
  a tone stack finds none of them, which is a refund and a one-star review
  rather than a misunderstanding. "Guitar amp" is also among the most
  contested search terms in the category, so a descriptive name ranks
  nowhere while a distinctive one owns its own search outright. And a purely
  descriptive name is worthless as a mark — "guitaramp" cannot be protected,
  and Apple treats generic category terms in an app name as keyword stuffing.
  The instinct behind it is sound; the subtitle is where it belongs.
- **jj-tulsa-sound** — "the Tulsa Sound" is the name of a *scene*, which puts
  it in the same bin as **Red Dirt** above: it reads as a genre label and
  implies affiliation with a movement. It also makes the first requirement
  worse rather than better, since the extra word adds no second meaning for
  the majority who do not know Cale, and `sound` is a category word that
  belongs in the subtitle. Three parts breaks the `jj-x` family shape and
  crowds the panel wordmark.
- **jj-breeze-guitar** — collides with the shipped sibling. Two store listings
  differing only by an appended category word read as "the guitar version of
  jj-breeze" — the same effect, aimed at guitar — which is not what this is,
  and confusing your own two products in search is the worst place to spend a
  name. `guitar` is also the descriptive term ruled out above: the most
  contested search term in the category, worthless as a mark, and read by
  Apple as keyword stuffing in a title.
- **Travis** — the alternating-bass thumb pattern is *Travis picking*, and the
  name is tempting as the next step from `jj-thumb`. It is Merle Travis: a
  person, ruled out for the same reason as **Slow Hand**.
- **jj-drive** — four problems. `Drive` is already one of the four blocks in
  this app, so the product name would collide with a control on its own panel.
  It names the wrong block and oversells it: the Drive range stops at the edge
  of breakup by design and the compressor is the heart of the chain, so a
  buyer reading "drive" expects a gain range and finds none — the
  **jj-guitaramp** expectation gap in miniature. "Drive" is among the most
  crowded words in audio plug-ins, so it is unwinnable in search the way
  **Slack** is. And to a non-player it reads as a car: motion and urgency,
  the opposite of the thesis — while carrying no Cale reference either, so
  unlike `jj-tulsa` it does not even pay off once.
- **jj-thumb-roll** — stacks four objections already recorded above. It fails
  the first requirement outright: "thumb roll" has no plain reading that says
  warm or unhurried, so it pays off neither for the player nor for anyone
  else. It takes the `jj-touch` platform objection and makes it worse, since
  on an iPhone and iPad app the two words together name the scroll gesture
  exactly. It takes `jj-thumbpick`'s length objection and makes it worse —
  ten characters after the prefix plus a second hyphen, where nine already
  crowded the panel wordmark. And it is the three-part shape ruled out for
  **jj-tulsa-sound** just above. It is not even unambiguous for players: a
  thumb-lead roll is banjo and fingerstyle vocabulary more than guitar
  vocabulary, and nowhere near as fixed a term as *Travis picking* or *palm
  mute*. The direction is sound and is kept — `jj-thumb` at #6 carries it in
  five letters with none of this.
- **jj-midnight-drive** — the compound is out for the same reason as
  `Night Drive` above, and worse: fourteen characters after the prefix, and
  *midnight drive* is if anything more squarely synthwave than *night drive*.
  Recorded here because an earlier note called it strictly worse than
  `jj-night-drive`, which was wrong on one axis — *midnight* carries the
  "After Midnight" reference that *night* does not. That is worth keeping,
  which is why `jj-midnight` is now in *Also in view*; it is only the `drive`
  half that has to go.
- **Relic** — real guitar-industry vocabulary for a deliberately aged
  instrument, which is exactly why it is unusable: Fender uses Relic as a
  product designation. The same trap as Bigsby and Dyna Comp above.
- **Tobacco** — tobacco burst is a classic dark finish and the word carries the
  right warmth, but it is the *Magnolia* problem again rather than a trademark
  one: a content-guidelines coin flip on a word that would be visible in the
  store screenshots. Not worth the risk for a finish name.
- **Shimmer** — in audio this is not a mood word, it is the name of an effect:
  the octave-up ambient reverb, as in Strymon BigSky's Shimmer mode and
  Valhalla Shimmer. Anyone reading it expects bright, ethereal and wide, and
  this plug-in is dark, rolled-off and does no pitch shifting at all. A worse
  misdirection than jj-haze's nod to Purple Haze, because that is a song and
  this is a product category — and it sits uncomfortably close to a
  competitor's product name.

### What the rename covered

Done, and it cost almost nothing, exactly as this section predicted while it
was still called *Cost of changing it*: no App Store record, no users with
saved presets, no remote. The five real decisions, now:

| What | Was | Is |
|---|---|---|
| AU subtype | `Jjt1` | `Jjm1` |
| AU component name | `Gerov: jj-tulsa` | `Gerov: jj-midnight` |
| Bundle IDs | `com.gerov.jjtulsa`, `.AUv3` | `com.gerov.jjmidnight`, `.AUv3` |
| App Group | `group.com.gerov.jjtulsa` | `group.com.gerov.jjmidnight` |
| IAP product | `com.gerov.jjtulsa.unlock` | `com.gerov.jjmidnight.unlock` |

The manufacturer code stays `Grov` — it identifies the developer, not the
product, and jj-breeze shares it.

Everything else was mechanical: directories and filenames `JJTulsa*` →
`JJMidnight*`, the identifiers with them, and `project.pbxproj` regenerated
from `project.yml` by `xcodegen` rather than edited. Done by hand alongside:

- the icon wordmark — `J.J.Midnight` in `icon/make-icon.swift`, re-rendered.
  The size is no longer hard-coded: twelve characters overran the canvas
  where nine fitted, so the script now shrinks the wordmark to a fixed
  maximum width. Anything that fits still gets the old 150 pt.
- the panel wordmark — `Text("j.j.midnight")` in `JJMidnightMainView.swift`
- the model plate — `MODEL JJM-1` in the same file
- the site under `docs/`

Two consequences worth knowing:

- **The UserDefaults keys moved** with the bundle prefix — `jjtulsa.theme`,
  `jjtulsa.installDate.v*`, `jjtulsa.userPresets.v*`, `jjtulsa.accessState.v*`
  and `jjtulsa.effectAllowed.v*` are all `jjmidnight.*` now. Any local state
  on a development device is orphaned, which resets the seven-day trial clock
  there. Harmless with no users; it is exactly the breakage that makes this
  impossible after launch.
- **`docs/comp.md` still says "The Tulsa squash"** for the compressor setting.
  Left deliberately: it names the musical idiom, not the product, the way the
  positioning section does. Worth a second look if the scene reference is
  unwanted on a public page at all.

Preset names are unaffected.

**After launch a further rename costs** the App Store identity, the IAP, and
every buyer's saved presets — at which point it is effectively impossible.

## Positioning

The engine is a generic vintage clean / low-gain guitar chain. The artist-era
tones are **presets on top of it**, not the product. Same build effort, larger
market, less legal surface, and preset packs are then the way to grow it
rather than more DSP.

Two audiences, one chain:

- **The laid-back end.** Compressor → mild breakup → tremolo → slapback is the
  early-70s Tulsa signal chain. This audience knows exactly what it wants and
  skews older — and, being pedal-and-tube-amp players, is not who mostly buys
  AUv3.
- **The other end.** The *same* chain is the current bedroom-pop and ambient
  guitar sound. That audience is 20–35, and it is the one that actually buys
  iPad plug-ins.

Which is why the store copy should describe the sound ("warm, compressed
vintage guitar tone") rather than the era or the scene, and let the presets
speak to each end: *Laid Back*, *Escondido*, *Magnolia* for one; *Faded*,
*Cassette*, *Night Drive* for the other.

## Naming rules for copy and presets

- **Preset names:** song titles and places, yes. Artist or band names, never.
  Titles are too short to carry copyright, which is what makes them the
  tolerable end of this; an artist's name in a product is a false-endorsement
  problem.
- **When in doubt, shorten.** *Call Me The Breeze* became **Breeze** and
  *After Midnight* became **Midnight**. Both still point at the same feel,
  and a one-word name is much harder to read as a claim about a particular
  recording than the full title is. The full title buys nothing the short
  form does not — anyone who knows the song still hears it, which is the
  whole `jj-breeze` trick above — and it carries all of the risk. Prefer the
  short form when writing new presets, not just when auditing old ones.
- **Never claim** a preset reproduces a particular recording. They are original
  settings arrived at by ear.
- **The App Store listing is a much smaller surface than the app.** None of the
  song titles belong in the store name, subtitle, keywords, or description.
  Paraphrase instead: "inspired by the laid-back sound of the early 70s".
- **Apple's content rules are a separate axis from trademark.** The preset
  originally called *Cocaine* was renamed to *Magnolia* — not for trademark
  reasons but because Review Guideline 1.1.6 covers references to illegal
  drugs, and the preset name is visible in screenshots. An avoidable coin
  flip. Watch for this class of problem independently of the legal one.
